I am learning French, which gives me regular opportunities to be confused.
Even beer comes with homework: *une bière*, a feminine noun. The drink I
recognize. The article still needs supervision.

Then I came across a sentence by
[Buffon](https://athena.unige.ch/athena/buffon/buffon-discours-sur-le-style.pdf#page=5)
that clicked: *Le style est l’homme même.* “Style is the man himself,” in my
literal translation. He delivered the speech containing it at his reception
into the Académie française on August 25, 1753.

In that passage, Buffon distinguishes knowledge that can pass between writers
from the style of the work that holds it. I take from this a question about
my own writing: which choices make a piece recognizably mine? The subject I
choose, the detail I notice, the objection I take seriously, the sentence I
decide to cut. A page acquires personality through those decisions.

Another French phrase makes the ambition tangible: *le mot juste*, the right
word. The Bibliothèque nationale de France’s account of
[Flaubert’s revisions](https://classes.bnf.fr/pdf/Brouillons_Flaubert.pdf#page=3)
shows him changing *s’adossa* to *s’appuya*, then returning to the first verb.
In that scene, I would translate the phrases as “rested his back against a
tree” and “leaned against a tree.” One gives the posture a little more
precision. The choice changes what the reader sees.

I want that attention at every scale: a word, a paragraph, the shape of an
essay. My writing protocol draws selectively on six books about craft,
through published excerpts and author and publisher commentary. It turns their
advice into choices I can make while working. A rule earns its place by helping
me produce something clearer, more exact, or more alive.

The first choice is what the piece should give the reader. A distinction they
can use, an experience they can picture, a reason to reconsider something.
Before substantial writing, I set down the intended reader, the central
thought, and what supports it. A provisional question is enough to begin.
Planning should give the work direction without pretending its conclusion is
already settled.

A concrete case helps. Suppose a library requires an online account to reserve
a place at a workshop. I want to propose letting visitors join at the door.
This is a hypothetical example, rather than a report about a particular
library. It gives the broad topic of “access” a rule, an action, and people
whose needs may differ.

I can explain how the proposal removes the account requirement for those
visitors. Claiming it would increase attendance would require evidence. I
would need to know whether people abandon registration, whether the room has
space, and what organizers need to prepare. Each question helps determine the
claim I can defend. When using outside evidence, I keep its source beside the
assertion and preserve the conditions under which it holds.

Then I choose the form. A proposal should state the change early, explain the
problem, and address how the change would work. A reported story might follow
someone’s attempt to attend. An exploratory essay could examine the tension
between easy entry and reliable planning. The subject can support all three;
the purpose and available material determine which one I should write.

When the thought is still forming, I start smaller. Stephen King’s
[*On Writing*](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/On-Writing/Stephen-King/9781439193631)
cautions against inviting reactions too early. Anne Lamott’s
[account](https://annelamott.substack.com/p/so-you-want-to-write-a-memoir) of
*Bird by Bird* returns to small assignments and imperfect first drafts.
Together, those ideas suggest a useful beginning: write one passage with
enough freedom to discover something. I can explain the registration rule or
explore an objection before knowing where that paragraph belongs.

Once a draft exists, I inspect its actual structure. I give each paragraph a
short label describing its job, then read the labels in order. Three versions
of “why this matters” suggest the argument needs to move. An unfamiliar term
appearing before its explanation reveals a missing step. Zinsser’s
[question](https://theamericanscholar.org/writing-english-as-a-second-language/),
“What does the reader need to know next?”, helps me decide what follows.

An argument also needs an attempt to break it. For the workshop proposal, a
serious objection is that walk-ins could take places from people who reserved
them. That deserves an answer about capacity and admissions. If I cannot
answer it, I should narrow the proposal or reconsider it. Confidence comes
from being able to explain why the conclusion survives a fair challenge.

Only then does sustained work on the sentences make sense. Consider this
invented draft:

> The implementation of a more flexible registration process could facilitate
> an improvement in accessibility.

The nouns sound substantial, but the reader cannot tell what anyone would do.
Strunk and White’s
[*The Elements of Style*](https://www.pearson.com/en-gb/subject-catalog/p/elements-of-style-the-pearson-new-international-edition/P200000005508)
emphasizes concrete language and clear word order. Applied to the proposal,
those principles point toward:

> Let visitors join the workshop at the door if unreserved seats remain.

The revision names an action and a limit. It also replaces a vague aspiration
with a specific proposal, which is a substantive choice I must mean. Editing
can force me to decide what I am actually saying.

Cut the final condition and the sentence becomes shorter but changes the
policy. I keep those words. In *On Writing Well*, Zinsser’s
[clutter exercise](https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/readwrite/readwrite_ch52_26.html)
brackets possible cuts and lets the writer read the passage without them.
I use that comparison to protect meaning, rhythm, and voice. A necessary
qualification or illuminating example deserves space; a paragraph repeating
the setup may deserve none.

Precision also means distinguishing “possible” from “likely,” or “permits”
from “encourages.” Such words occupy the same grammatical space while making
different claims. I want to choose them with the thought in view. A technical
term may need repetition for consistency; swapping in synonyms merely for
variety can introduce confusion.

There is pleasure to consider too. Roy Peter Clark’s
[*Writing Tools*](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2006/fifty-writing-tools-quick-list/)
offers ways to shape emphasis and pace: move a consequential phrase to the
end of a sentence, connect an abstraction to a particular case, vary sentence
length as the thought develops. A longer sentence can hold a relationship
together through its qualifications. A short one can give it room to land.
I listen for that movement when reading a passage aloud.

I want a voice that is curious, direct, and composed, with room for wit and
an unexpected connection. Warmth should come from attention to the reader.
Originality can come from noticing what an explanation leaves out. An unusual
phrase is welcome when it makes the subject more visible; I have less use for
one that asks the reader to admire the phrase.

Taste needs exercise. In discussing *The Art of X-Ray Reading*, Clark suggests
[rereading](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2016/the-secret-to-being-a-better-writer-re-read-your-own-work/)
our own work with distance. I can extend that practice to a paragraph I admire:
name its effect, inspect its verbs and turns, change one feature, and compare.
Then try the technique on fresh material. That gives me a reason for preferring
one version, and a better chance of recognizing when the same technique
would be wrong.

These standards apply however a piece is produced. Another reader or an AI
collaborator can question an argument and propose revisions. My core views
and direction remain mine. I judge a suggestion by what it does to the work
and take responsibility for what I publish.

Before finishing, I compare the title’s promise with the essay beneath it.
I check claims and quotations, resolve unfinished notes, and read for the
missing step or forced connection. The ending should arrive where the piece
has been heading. Further edits need a reason beyond exchanging two equally
good choices.

The companion essay,
<a href={import.meta.env.BASE_URL.replace(/\/$/, '') + '/writing/in-search-of-the-right-word'}>In Search of the Right Word</a>,
tells the story behind the protocol. Here, *le mot juste* names the attention
I want to bring to the page: find something worth saying, give it a form the
reader can follow, and keep looking until the words make the thought visible.
