I want to understand which assumption carries an argument, what evidence
could break it, and how the thing would actually work if someone built it.
Often, I want to be that someone.

I think of myself as a builder, researcher, and investor. My background
spans scientific approaches to public-market investing and venture
investing. That combination keeps bringing me to the boundary between
what can be tested with existing evidence and what somebody has to create
before the evidence can exist. I care about both. An explanation should
survive scrutiny; an unfamiliar idea deserves enough room to become
testable.

I am particularly interested when a conclusion is widely accepted but
the reasoning beneath it is surprisingly thin. Agreement can be earned.
It can also be inherited. I want to know which I am looking at before
building on it. And once a direction holds up, I want to turn it into
something that works. There is only so much satisfaction in identifying
a problem from a safe distance.

Yet I am not used to sharing much of this thinking. I have usually
thought of myself as an outsider: observing, analyzing, building,
somewhere beyond the edge of the frame. There is freedom in that
position. You can notice what the people in the picture take for
granted. You can also forget that nobody else can see what you have noticed.

I can also approach a personal decision like a portfolio optimization
problem: revise the estimates, adjust the constraints, search for a better
allocation. Except the asset being allocated is my own effort, and I keep
postponing the trade. If I require every uncertain input to be settled
first, an impressive amount of analysis can produce an allocation of zero.
A sufficiently elaborate plan can become a respectable way of doing nothing.

Writing here is a way to bring that thinking into the world, where it can
become useful to someone else and come back changed.

Writing helps me think because a sentence makes me commit. A thought in my
head can quietly change its meaning whenever it encounters a difficulty.
On the page, the gap between two steps stays visible until I do something
about it. Sometimes the missing step can be supplied. Sometimes the whole
idea has to go. Either outcome is progress.

Sharing takes the process one step further. Someone else can find the
assumption I stopped questioning, offer an example I could never have
encountered, or follow a connection further than I did. There is also
something more personal at stake. The questions a person returns to, the
things they find funny, the evidence that changes their mind: these reveal
an internal world that a biography barely touches. I want to let people
into mine. I hope a few will recognize something there and let me into
theirs. That seems a promising way for a deep connection to begin.

The essays here will follow those interests through science, technology,
markets, and the work of making things. Some questions will concern what
is true; others, what is worth building or backing. I want these pages to
be useful both to someone choosing a first serious problem and to someone
who has spent years working on one. A question from
outside a field can expose an assumption; an answer from inside it can
explain why that assumption survived. I would like both people in the
conversation.

Some of my appetite for unlikely projects comes from home. I was born and
raised in Linzi, in Zibo, Shandong Province, China, the old capital of Qi.
Qi's founding ruler, [Jiang Ziya](https://ctext.org/shiji/qi-tai-gong-shi-jia/zh),
offers an encouraging precedent for anyone whose ambitions are developing
faster than their achievements. In the traditional story, an elderly
Jiang fishes by the Wei River until he encounters King Wen, the worthy
ruler who recognizes his ability. Jiang goes on to help establish the
Zhou dynasty and receives Qi to govern.

I like to think I am a late bloomer in the same tradition. It is a generous
historical benchmark.

The fish I have in mind belongs to a different story. In the *Zhuangzi*,
[Ren Gongzi](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/what-comes-from-without/ens)
makes an enormous hook and uses fifty oxen as bait. He fishes in the
Eastern Sea for a year without a catch. Then a huge fish takes the hook
and plunges into the depths: 錎没而下. The sea heaves; the waves rise like
mountains. Here, at last, is an event large enough to justify the equipment.

I am building toward a 錎没而下 moment of my own. Jiang was fishing for a
ruler worth serving; I am fishing for a legacy. I want to make something
consequential enough to leave my existence engraved in the universe:
a discovery, a company, a body of work that changes what other people
can do and lasts beyond me. That is a large ambition to put next to a
first essay. It also explains why I care so much about where to put my
effort. The danger is allowing the size of the imagined result to become
an excuse for never casting the line.

Linzi supplied an earlier ambition too. It appears in the earliest
written accounts of [cuju](https://editorial.fifamuseum.com/en/read/origins-cuju-in-china/),
the ancient Chinese game of kicking a ball. Cuju belongs to football's
early history; modern association football developed separately in Britain.
As a child, that connection made me proud: invention belonged to the
place I came from. Something could begin there and matter far away.

China's men's football team has since provided plenty of opportunities
to practice patience. I once considered myself a glimmer of hope for
its future. Unfortunately, doctors told me as a child that I should avoid
intense sports, including football. My hypothetical international career
therefore remains undefeated.

The impulse underneath that childhood confidence has survived: I want
to participate in making something possible. This site gives that
impulse a modest place to start, and gives my thoughts somewhere to
meet people beyond my own head.

It also gives me an excuse to build a small piece of science fiction.
I love the genre. The site imagines a remote observatory: white and deep green
in daylight, black and silver after sunset, with a sun above the
terrain and restrained lines around the prose. A personal website
should be allowed to reveal that its owner has opinions about an
imaginary planet.

The landscape gives shape to a question I keep returning to: how much
of the terrain must I understand before choosing a direction? A better
map can change a decision, but making the map can also become a way
to postpone it. I still have to act before I understand everything.

For anyone curious about the construction, the site is built with
Astro and MDX, styled with Tailwind CSS v4, and deployed on Vercel.
Georgia carries the body text; the headings use the system sans-serif
stack. KaTeX renders the mathematics, and Shiki highlights code at
build time in both light and dark themes. Sidenotes sit inside the
reading column on wide screens and join the flow on smaller ones.
The pages are static HTML, with SVG and CSS for decoration. The theme
toggle supplies the only client-side JavaScript. The machinery leaves
room for the words, and for the occasional equation that earns its place.

For now, this is where I step into the frame. You can see what I am
thinking, including the parts I have yet to work out. If something here
connects with a question of your own, I hope we get to explore it together.
