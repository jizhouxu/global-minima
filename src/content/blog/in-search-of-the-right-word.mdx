In [*Interstellar*](https://www.paramountpictures.com/movies/interstellar),
a crew leaves a failing Earth to search for a future among the stars.
What draws me to that premise is the combination of uncertainty and purpose.
They have a reason to go before they know where they can land.

An empty page offers a much smaller version of that uncertainty. There may
be a thought worth reaching. There is also a lot of open space.

I wanted help exploring it. More precisely, I wanted to work with AI on a
protocol that would make good writing more deliberate: clearer thinking,
better choices, enough personality to make the result worth reading.
The core views and direction would remain mine. The collaboration would
give me more ways to develop and question their expression.

I began with a short file of style instructions drawn from OpenAI developer
guidance. It asked for plain language, connected paragraphs, and an early
sense of purpose. It also named habits to avoid: inflated diction, canned
transitions, and sentences that announce an idea instead of expressing it.

From there, I asked AI to draw on six books about craft and distill
principles we could use on an actual draft. We consulted published excerpts,
the writers’ own explanations, and publisher material. The useful work was
turning advice into something observable. “Be clear” needed a test.
“Have a voice” needed decisions about what that voice would notice and
how it would reason.

Here is a tightened version of the brief, condensed from our exchange:

> Build a general writing protocol for taste, personality, structure, and
> language. Turn the useful lessons from these books into techniques we can
> apply. Explain when a rule needs an exception. Judge the resulting work
> by what it gives the reader, whoever helped produce it.

That last sentence matters. The protocol needed to work when I wrote alone,
when AI produced a draft, and when a piece emerged through conversation.
Its subject was the quality of the writing.

AI did much of the source gathering and drafting. I supplied the direction,
asked for broader standards, and pressed on sentences whose polish exceeded
their clarity. Those are different contributions, and both are visible in
the work. A model can offer a structure I would not have tried. I can decide
that the structure serves the wrong purpose.

One useful part of the collaboration was comparing advice that seemed to
pull in different directions. Give the reader the point early, but allow an
essay to discover something. Cut unnecessary words, but preserve the detail
that makes an idea intelligible. Let a draft be awkward, then revise it
carefully.

These became conditions in the protocol. If I am recommending an action,
the reader should find that recommendation near the beginning. If I am
exploring a question, the opening should make the question worth following.
The same rule cannot choose both openings for me. I have to understand
what the piece is doing.

That made the conversation more interesting. We could discuss a particular
choice: whether an example arrived too late, whether a cut removed a
condition, whether a confident sentence had outrun its evidence. A request
for better writing became a request to examine how the writing worked.

The corresponding revision prompt can be just as direct. This is another
condensed reconstruction, rather than a verbatim transcript:

> Apply the protocol to this draft. Find sentences the reader has to
> reconstruct, claims stronger than their support, and sections that repeat
> rather than develop the thought. Propose specific edits and explain what
> each changes. Preserve my intended meaning and flag any substantive change.

I want that final distinction to remain visible. Replacing a vague verb
may clarify a sentence. Removing “under these conditions” may change the
argument. Both edits can make a line look smoother. Only a reading attentive
to meaning can tell what has happened.

The protocol also has room for pleasure. I asked for a personality:
curiosity, a clear point of view, fair treatment of an objection, a little
humor when the subject allows it. A useful image should let the reader see
something they had missed. A surprising connection should survive a closer
look. Accuracy gives us a basis for taking those chances.

This is where the spaceflight image becomes useful to me again. The search
needs imagination and instruments. Imagination suggests a direction.
The instruments help establish what is actually there. In writing, a
sentence can be an invitation to investigate; it still needs to survive
the investigation.

There is a familiar temptation to settle the value of an AI-assisted piece
by identifying the tool involved. Reid Hoffman addresses that in
[“The Pangloss of Pangram”](https://reidhoffman.substack.com/p/the-pangloss-of-pangram),
his August 6, 2026 essay. He argues that a judgment about where the words
came from does not settle whether the work deserves attention. He also
insists that the person publishing it must have read and weighed it.

One sentence captures the responsibility: “Regardless of the tools used,
a human being must mean what is said.”

That fits what I want from this collaboration. I care about the finished
artifact: the thought it offers, the accuracy of its claims, the experience
of reading it. The process matters where it affects those qualities and
the honesty of what I present. A beautifully expressed invention passed
off as a personal memory would fail that standard.

I also want writing to remain a way of thinking. If AI finds a weak
inference, I want to understand the weakness. If it suggests another
conclusion, I want to consider it. The possibility of changing my mind
is part of the value. The decision to change it is mine.

The resulting protocol connects taste with practice. It asks what a piece
is for, gives it a form, tests the argument, and works down to the sentence.
It includes ways to read closely, learn from an admired passage, and carry
a useful technique into fresh material. The companion essay,
<a href={import.meta.env.BASE_URL.replace(/\/$/, '') + '/blog/le-mot-juste'}>Le Mot Juste</a>,
describes the protocol and puts it to work.

There is no final set of coordinates for good writing. A different subject
will require another choice of pace, another example, another way in.
What I hope to gain is a better way to search: enough freedom to follow
an unexpected thought, enough care to find out whether it holds.

The next page is still open. I want to give the reader a reason to come along.
