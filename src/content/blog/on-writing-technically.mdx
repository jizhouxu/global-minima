A sentence can be perfectly grammatical and leave the reader no wiser. Before
I worry about its elegance, I want to know what it gives them: a distinction
they can use, an experience they can picture, a reason to reconsider something.
That is where my writing process begins.

I start with a few plain lines about the piece. Who is reading? What do I want
them to understand? What have I noticed that makes this worth writing? An
unsettled question is a sufficient beginning. I do not need to force a
conclusion before examining the material, but I need a reason to examine it.

Then I gather something more definite than a topic. “Access” is a topic. A rule
that requires someone to create an online account before attending a library
workshop gives an essay something to investigate. Throughout this piece, I
will use that hypothetical rule as a writing exercise; it describes no
particular library.

Suppose I want to argue for allowing people to join the workshop at the door.
I can explain how that would remove the account requirement. Claiming it would
increase attendance would require evidence. I would want to know whether
people abandon registration, whether the room has space, and what organizers
need to prepare. Those questions help determine the claim I can defend.

When using evidence, I keep the source beside the assertion it supports. I
check what was measured, over what period, and under which conditions. A
quotation needs its surrounding argument. A number needs enough context to
mean something. Where the evidence stops, the prose should let the reader see
me making an inference.

The next choice is shape. A proposal about registration should present the
change, explain the problem, and address how it would work. A reported story
might follow someone's attempt to attend. An exploratory essay could begin
with the tension between making entry easy and planning an event reliably.
The same subject can support all three. I choose the form that serves the
piece's purpose and the material I actually have.

If that choice feels premature, I write one useful passage. Anne Lamott's
[advice to work in small pieces and permit an imperfect first draft](https://annelamott.substack.com/p/so-you-want-to-write-a-memoir)
offers a manageable way in. I might explain the rule, examine one objection,
or describe a verified detail. A rough paragraph can expose the question an
outline has failed to find.

Once there is a draft, I give each paragraph a brief label describing its job.
Read in order, those labels reveal the structure I have actually written.
Three versions of “why this matters” probably mean the piece is waiting to
begin. An unfamiliar term appearing before its explanation points to a missing
step. William Zinsser's question,
[“What does the reader need to know next?”](https://theamericanscholar.org/writing-english-as-a-second-language/),
is a useful guide to the repair.

An argument also needs a serious attempt to break it. For the workshop
proposal, a fair objection is that walk-ins could take places from people who
reserved them. That concern deserves an answer about capacity and admissions.
If I cannot answer it, I should narrow the proposal or reconsider it. An
objection earns space when resolving it changes what the reader can reasonably
conclude.

Now I can work on the sentences. Consider this invented draft:

> The implementation of a more flexible registration process could facilitate
> an improvement in accessibility.

The nouns sound substantial, but the reader cannot tell what anyone would do.
Roy Peter Clark's advice to
[bring subjects and verbs forward and choose concrete details](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2006/fifty-writing-tools-quick-list/)
suggests a better direction. Given the proposal above, I could write:

> Let visitors join the workshop at the door if unreserved seats remain.

The revision names an action and a limit. It also replaces a vague aspiration
with a specific proposal, which is a substantive choice I must mean. Editing
can clarify an idea by forcing me to decide what it is.

A further cut would produce “Let visitors join the workshop at the door.”
That sentence is shorter, but it has lost the capacity condition. I keep the
words that protect the meaning. I am happier cutting an entire paragraph of
repeated setup than shaving a necessary qualification from a claim.

Clarity still leaves room for pleasure. I want a precise detail, an unexpected
but fitting word, a sentence whose movement suits its thought. Clark also
[recommends varying sentence and paragraph length to control pace](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2006/fifty-writing-tools-quick-list/).
I can let a longer sentence carry a relationship through its qualifications,
then give the consequence a sentence of its own. The pause matters. A page of
equally clipped sentences can make every idea sound equally urgent.

Another reader can show me where that intention fails. An editor, a colleague,
or an AI collaborator can question the argument and propose revisions. My core
views and direction remain mine; I decide what to adopt. The useful test of
any suggestion is what it does to the piece.

Before finishing, I check the title's promise against the work beneath it. I
verify claims and quotations, resolve unfinished notes, and look for certainty
that crept in during revision. Then I read for continuity: whether one thought
leads into the next, whether the important detail has room, whether the ending
arrives where the piece has been heading.

explains the principles behind this approach. To put them to work, I need only

does, and revise until the reader can see what I mean.
