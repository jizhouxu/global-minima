I’m learning French, and one sentence gives me a reason to linger:
[*Le style est l’homme même*](https://athena.unige.ch/athena/buffon/buffon-discours-sur-le-style.pdf#page=5).
“Style is the man himself,” in my literal translation. Buffon delivered the
speech containing it at his reception into the Académie française on
August 25, 1753.

The claim is larger than a preference for certain words. In that passage,
Buffon distinguishes knowledge that can pass from one writer to another from
the style of the work that holds it. I take from this a question for my own
writing: which choices make a piece recognizably mine?

There is the subject I choose, the detail I notice, the objection I take
seriously. There is the moment I decide an explanation needs an example, or
that an attractive sentence has no place in the argument. Those decisions
give the page a personality before I choose an adjective.

Another French phrase brings the question down to the scale of a word:
*le mot juste*, the right word. The Bibliothèque nationale de France’s
[account of Flaubert’s revisions](https://classes.bnf.fr/pdf/Brouillons_Flaubert.pdf#page=3)
shows him changing *s’adossa* to *s’appuya*, then returning to the first verb.
I would translate the phrases in that scene as “rested his back against a
tree” and “leaned against a tree.” The first choice makes the character’s
posture more precise.

I like the attention behind that search. “Possible,” “likely,” and “certain”
can fit into the same grammatical space while making different claims.
“Permits” and “encourages” describe different relationships. Choosing between
them means deciding what I think is happening. A word can force a thought
to become more exact.

That is the ambition behind the writing protocol I have been developing.
It began with a short set of style instructions: use plain language, connect
the paragraphs, make the point clear early, and avoid canned phrases. We
expanded it with lessons from six books about craft, consulting excerpts,
author commentary, and publisher materials. The aim is to make those lessons
available at the moment a draft needs them.

An earlier version of this essay offered a useful test: “A decision may
need its answer in the first sentence.”

I meant: “If you are recommending a course of action, state the recommendation
first.”

The revision identifies the task and tells someone what to do. The original
compresses the thought until the reader has to reconstruct it. A decision
does not have a first sentence. A document recommending a decision does.
We had written advice about clarity that needed clarifying.

That correction also points to a larger distinction. A recommendation, an
explanation, and a reflective essay ask different things of their openings.
A recommendation should tell the reader what action is proposed. An
explanation should establish what needs explaining. An essay can begin with
a French sentence, provided it gives the reader a reason to follow it.

I want the protocol to help choose among those forms. Applying the same
opening to every piece would erase useful possibilities. The subject and
purpose should determine how the work unfolds.

They should also determine what deserves space. William Zinsser’s
[exercise for removing clutter](https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/readwrite/readwrite_ch52_26.html)
brackets possible cuts and asks the writer to read the passage without them.
The decision remains open. That is a better test than assuming every shorter
version improves the work.

A necessary qualification may take several words. A concrete example may
take a paragraph. Both can make an argument easier to understand. I want
to remove the material that makes the reader work without giving them
something in return, while keeping the steps that make the thought accessible.

There is a danger in applying all these standards too early. Anne Lamott’s
[advice to work in small pieces and allow an imperfect draft](https://annelamott.substack.com/p/so-you-want-to-write-a-memoir)
gives an unfinished idea room to develop. One passage can reveal what the
piece is about. Once there is a passage, I can test its claims and see
what a reader still needs.

Then revision can move from the whole to the sentence. Does the argument
have a missing step? Does the middle introduce anything the opening did not
already say? Has the best example arrived too late? A beautiful line in
the wrong place can still make a piece harder to follow.

Roy Peter Clark’s
[*Writing Tools* offers ways to shape emphasis and pace](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2006/fifty-writing-tools-quick-list/):
move a consequential phrase to the end of a sentence, connect an abstraction
to a particular case, vary the length of sentences as the thought develops.
These are choices to try against the passage. Their value lies in the
effect they produce.

The effect I want includes pleasure. A clear page can still surprise.
A well-chosen detail can change the scale of an idea. A sentence can pause
just long enough for its consequence to register. I want room for wit,
an unexpected connection, and the occasional image that makes the familiar
look strange. The connection has to withstand examination; the image has
to help us see.

For this writing, that sensibility means curiosity with a point of view.
It means explaining why something follows, giving a serious objection a
fair hearing, and being precise about uncertainty. I want warmth without
flattery. When I sound confident, the reader should be able to see why.

Taste needs practice too. In discussing *The Art of X-Ray Reading*, Clark
extends close reading to
[revisiting our own earlier work](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2016/the-secret-to-being-a-better-writer-re-read-your-own-work/).
Distance can make an old choice newly visible. I can ask which observation
still holds, which phrase now feels forced, and what I would change.

The protocol turns that habit into an exercise: choose a short passage,
name its effect, and examine how it works. Change one feature and compare
the result. Try the technique on fresh material. The aim is to learn why
one version works better than another.

These standards apply to a sentence written alone, an AI-generated draft,
or a piece shaped through conversation. I care about what the finished
work gives the reader. In my own work, the core views and direction remain
mine; help with research, drafting, or revision should make them clearer
and expose what needs reconsidering. The quality of the result includes
the accuracy of its claims and whether I mean what it says.

The companion essay,
<a href={import.meta.env.BASE_URL.replace(/\/$/, '') + '/blog/on-writing-technically'}>How I Write</a>,
puts the principles to work on a draft. The protocol should keep developing
as actual revisions reveal what helps.

For now, *le mot juste* names the attention I want to bring to the page.
Find what is worth saying. Give it a form the reader can follow. Keep
looking until the words make the thought visible.
