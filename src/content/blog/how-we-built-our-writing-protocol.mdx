If I were putting an AI aboard a spacecraft, I would want it to question the
flight plan. I would also want to know who could change the destination.

That is roughly the arrangement I want for my writing. The core views and
direction are mine. AI is a copilot with room to research, challenge an
assumption, propose a structure, and work on the sentences. I remain responsible
for deciding what I believe and what I am prepared to publish.

To make that arrangement useful, we needed something more specific than a
request to write well.

I started with a short file of style instructions drawn from OpenAI developer
guidance: use plain language, connect the paragraphs, make the point clear early,
and avoid canned phrases. It was a useful beginning. It could catch an inflated
verb or an unnecessary announcement. It said much less about finding the point
of an essay, deciding what evidence it needed, or knowing when a graceful
sentence had changed the thought.

So I asked my AI collaborator to help build it into a writing protocol. We used
six books as reference points, researching published excerpts, the authors' own
explanations, and publisher materials. The task was to turn useful advice into
decisions we could make on an actual draft.

The first decision was to separate discovery from judgment.

Anne Lamott's *Bird by Bird* makes the beginning manageable: take on a small
piece of work and allow an imperfect draft. Stephen King's *On Writing* adds
another useful distinction: protect the early work from premature reactions,
then revise with an intended reader in mind. Together, these suggest a place
for an idea to be unfinished before we ask whether it is ready for someone else.
[Lamott on her book's central advice](https://annelamott.substack.com/p/so-you-want-to-write-a-memoir),
[King's publisher guide](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/On-Writing/Stephen-King/9781439193631).

For our protocol, this became a small instruction with a large consequence:
when the thought is unclear, work on one passage before polishing the whole
essay. AI can help explore that passage. Fluency alone does not tell us that the
underlying question has been settled.

The next decision concerned what to remove.

*The Elements of Style* gives us tests for sentence construction: keep related
words together, give a paragraph a coherent job, and remove needless language.
Its advice on economy leaves room for useful detail and long sentences.
[Strunk's original text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37134/37134-h/37134-h.htm),
[the Strunk and White edition's contents](https://www.pearson.com/en-gb/subject-catalog/p/elements-of-style-the-pearson-new-international-edition/P200000005508).

William Zinsser's *On Writing Well* offers a particularly useful editing
exercise: bracket possible clutter, read the passage without it, and leave the
decision with the writer. That gives an AI editor a concrete assignment. Propose
the cut. Explain what disappears. Let me decide whether the sentence has become
clearer or has lost something I meant.
[Zinsser's “Clutter” excerpt](https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/readwrite/readwrite_ch52_26.html).

Consider an illustrative sentence: “AI will improve my writing.” A more
defensible version might be: “I want AI to help me find the missing steps in an
argument.” The revision is longer. It also names a purpose I can evaluate. If
the missing step is still missing, a polished paragraph has not done the job.

Roy Peter Clark's two books widened the protocol beyond cutting.
*Writing Tools* supplied ways to shape emphasis and movement: place a
consequential phrase where it will land, connect an abstraction to an example,
and give the reader something to discover in the middle.
[Clark's original tool list](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2006/fifty-writing-tools-quick-list/).

*The Art of X-Ray Reading* supplied a way to keep learning. Take a passage that
works and examine how it produces its effect. For our purposes, that becomes a
controlled exercise: change one feature, compare the result, then try the
underlying technique on a different subject.
[Book description](https://www.hachette.co.uk/titles/roy-peter-clark/the-art-of-x-ray-reading/9780316282147/),
[Clark on slow reading](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2015/the-power-of-slow-reading-in-fast-times/).

This matters for the kind of voice I want to develop. I want to state a view
without performing certainty, explain how a conclusion follows, and give a
serious objection enough room to be understood. A number should help a reader
judge scale. A recommendation should make its consequences visible. When I am
uncertain, the prose should locate the uncertainty precisely.

Those choices give the protocol a personality. They also leave room for the
subject. An explanation may need a patient example. A decision may need its
answer in the first sentence. An essay may need a little distance before the
reader can see where it is going.

Some advice therefore needed conditions. Active voice is a useful default;
passive voice sometimes keeps attention on the thing being affected. A short
sentence can clarify a difficult step. A longer one can hold a relationship
together. Repetition can waste space, or it can keep a technical term stable
while the argument develops.

We wrote those conditions into the guide. A rule earns its place by helping us
make a choice. It should not turn every piece into the same piece.

The question of AI authorship belongs here, because it asks what all this work
is for. I care about the finished artifact: whether it has a thought worth
engaging with, whether the claims hold up, whether the reader can follow it,
and whether I mean what it says. AI participation alone cannot answer those
questions.

Reid Hoffman makes a closely related point in his August 6, 2026 essay on AI
writing and authorship. He argues that a judgment about the tools used does not
settle the value of the prose, while insisting that the named author must have
read and weighed it. One sentence earns a place in our protocol's explanation:
“Regardless of the tools used, a human being must mean what is said.”
[Hoffman's essay](https://reidhoffman.substack.com/p/the-pangloss-of-pangram).

For me, that standard includes truthfulness about the work itself. Invented
evidence weakens an artifact even when the sentences are beautiful. A fabricated
memory would misrepresent me. An argument I cannot explain would deserve more
work before it carried my name. The quality I am after includes that
responsibility.

I also want writing to help me think. An AI collaborator should be able to
expose a weak inference or suggest a view I have overlooked. I can change my
mind. What matters is that I consider the change and make it mine, rather than
letting a revision slide it into the text.

The resulting protocol moves from direction and evidence through drafting,
challenge, arrangement, and editing. We linked it from the repository's working
instructions so it can guide the next artifact. The
<a href={import.meta.env.BASE_URL.replace(/\/$/, '') + '/blog/on-writing-technically'}>practical companion to this essay</a>
sets out how to use that process.

This is a starting protocol. I am not claiming that writing it has already
proved its value. That will depend on the pieces it helps us produce and the
revisions I choose to keep. The guide should change as those decisions teach us
something.

Back aboard the hypothetical ship, I would welcome a copilot that notices a
fault in the course. I would want to hear its reasoning. Then I would choose
where we go.
